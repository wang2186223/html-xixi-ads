### chapter 1
The Dog Who Waited

For three years, Ethan Walker had asked his parents for a dog. He asked on his birthday. He asked at Christmas. He asked every time they walked past the pet store in town. And every time, the answer was the same.

"A dog is a big responsibility," his mother would say. "You have to feed it, walk it, train it, and love it every single day, even when it's raining and you'd rather stay in bed."

"I can do that," Ethan always promised.

"When you're twelve," his father finally said. "If you can show us you're ready."

On the morning of his twelfth birthday, Ethan's parents drove him to the Hillside Animal Shelter. Ethan could hardly breathe with excitement. Inside, rows of dogs barked, wagged their tails, and pressed their noses against the fences. There were tiny puppies, fluffy white dogs, and a big golden retriever who jumped up and down like a rubber ball.

But Ethan walked past all of them. At the very end of the hallway, in the last kennel, a medium-sized brown dog with one floppy ear sat quietly in the corner. He did not bark. He did not jump. He just watched Ethan with sad, patient eyes.

"That's Biscuit," said Ms. Lopez, the shelter volunteer. "He's about four years old. His owner was an elderly man who moved into a care home and couldn't take him. Biscuit has been here for six months. Most families want puppies, so people usually walk right past him."

Ethan knelt down by the fence. Biscuit stood up slowly, walked over, and gently licked his fingers.

"This one," Ethan said. "I want this one."

Biscuit came home that afternoon. At first, he was nervous. He hid under the kitchen table and flinched at loud noises. He barely ate his food. On the first night, he cried softly by the front door.

"He misses his old home," Ethan's mother said. "Give him time."

Ethan gave him time. He sat on the kitchen floor every evening and read his homework out loud, so Biscuit could get used to his voice. He woke up early every morning to take him for walks, even when it was cold and dark. He learned how to brush Biscuit's fur and clean his ears. Slowly, little by little, Biscuit began to change. He started wagging his tail when Ethan came downstairs. He started sleeping at the foot of Ethan's bed. And one day, he came out from under the table and dropped a tennis ball at Ethan's feet.

"He wants to play!" Ethan shouted. His parents smiled at each other.

But there was one strange thing about Biscuit. Every afternoon at exactly three-thirty, he would go to the front window and stare out at the street. He would sit there, perfectly still, for almost an hour. Then he would sigh and walk away.

"What is he waiting for?" Ethan wondered.

One Saturday, Ethan called Ms. Lopez at the shelter and asked her about it.

"His old owner was named Mr. Harold Jenkins," she said. "I remember he told us that every afternoon, he and Biscuit walked to the bus stop to meet his granddaughter when she came home from school. Maybe Biscuit is still waiting for that walk."

That night, Ethan could not stop thinking about Mr. Jenkins. He imagined an old man alone in a care home, missing his best friend. And he imagined Biscuit, waiting at the window every day for someone who never came.

"Mom," he said at breakfast the next morning, "do you think we could find out where Mr. Jenkins lives now?"

With help from Ms. Lopez, they found the answer. Mr. Jenkins lived at Sunny Meadows Care Home, only twenty minutes away. Ethan's mother called the care home and explained everything. The manager was delighted.

"Pets are welcome to visit," she said. "And Mr. Jenkins talks about his dog all the time."

The following Saturday, Ethan, his mother, and Biscuit walked through the front doors of Sunny Meadows. The building smelled like fresh flowers and coffee. In a sunny room by the garden, an old man with white hair sat in an armchair, reading a newspaper.

Biscuit froze. His ears went up. His nose twitched.

Then he pulled the leash right out of Ethan's hand and ran.

"Biscuit?" the old man said, dropping his newspaper. "Biscuit, is that you?"

Biscuit jumped into his lap, whining and wagging his tail so hard that his whole body shook. Mr. Jenkins wrapped his arms around the dog and laughed, and tears ran down his wrinkled cheeks.

"Oh, my good boy," he whispered. "My good, good boy."

Ethan felt a lump in his throat. For a moment, he worried. What if Biscuit loved Mr. Jenkins more? What if Biscuit wanted to stay?

But after a few minutes, Biscuit climbed down, walked back to Ethan, and leaned against his leg. Then he looked back at Mr. Jenkins, as if to say: "Look, I have a new friend."

Mr. Jenkins wiped his eyes and smiled at Ethan. "You've been taking good care of him. I can tell. His coat is shiny, and he looks happy." He held out a shaking hand. "Thank you, young man. I worried about him every single day."

Ethan shook his hand. "He waits by the window every afternoon," he said. "At three-thirty. I think he's been waiting for you."

Mr. Jenkins laughed softly. "That was our time. We walked to the bus stop to meet my granddaughter, Rosie. She's in college now, far away."

Ethan had an idea. "What if we came to visit you? Every Saturday? Biscuit could see you, and you could see him."

The old man's face lit up like the sun. "I would like that very much."

From then on, every Saturday afternoon, Ethan and Biscuit visited Sunny Meadows. Mr. Jenkins taught Ethan new tricks for Biscuit, like how to shake hands and how to roll over. He told stories about Biscuit as a puppy, chewing up slippers and chasing butterflies in the garden. Soon, other residents started joining them. An old woman named Mrs. Kim brought dog treats she had baked herself. A retired soldier named Mr. Brown taught Biscuit to sit up straight like he was saluting.

Biscuit became the most popular visitor at Sunny Meadows. The residents called him "the Saturday Dog."

And something else changed too. Biscuit stopped waiting at the window. At three-thirty each afternoon, instead of staring at the street, he would bring his leash to Ethan, and the two of them would walk together to the corner bus stop to meet Ethan's little sister, Grace, when she came home from school.

One evening, Ethan's father found him lying on the living room floor with Biscuit asleep beside him.

"You know," his father said, sitting down next to them, "when you asked for a dog, I thought you wanted something to play with. But you did more than that. You gave Biscuit a family. And you gave Mr. Jenkins his friend back."

Ethan rubbed Biscuit's floppy ear. The dog sighed happily in his sleep.

"I think Biscuit gave us something too," Ethan said.

His father smiled. "What's that?"

Ethan thought about the old man laughing in the sunny room, about Mrs. Kim's dog treats, and about all the Saturdays still to come.

"He showed us that love doesn't have to end," Ethan said. "Sometimes it just gets bigger."
