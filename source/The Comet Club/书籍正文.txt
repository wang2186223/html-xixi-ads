### chapter 1
The Comet Club

Every night, after finishing her homework, thirteen-year-old Ava Brooks climbed onto the flat roof of her family's garage with an old telescope and a notebook. The telescope had belonged to her mother, who had been a science teacher before she got sick and passed away three years ago. Ava still remembered sitting on this same roof with her, wrapped in a blanket, while her mother pointed out the stars.

"Every star has a story," her mother used to say. "And somewhere up there, there's one waiting just for you."

Ava had never told anyone at school about her nights on the roof. At Riverside Middle School, nobody talked about astronomy. They talked about video games, sports, and who had the newest phone. Ava was quiet and shy, and she worried that if she told people she loved stars, they would think she was strange.

But that changed in October, when Mr. Patel, the new science teacher, pinned a bright blue poster to the bulletin board outside his classroom.

"Astronomy Club. Every Thursday after school. Everyone welcome. Bring your curiosity!"

Ava stood in front of the poster for a long time. On Thursday, she almost walked straight past Mr. Patel's room. Then she remembered her mother's voice and stepped inside.

There were only three other students. There was Leo, a tall eighth grader who knew everything about rockets. There was Zoe, a sixth grader with bright purple glasses who asked so many questions that she barely stopped to breathe. And there was Daniel, who used a wheelchair and had a laptop covered in stickers of planets.

"Four members!" Mr. Patel said cheerfully. "That's a perfect start. Every great club begins small."

Zoe suggested a name. "We should be called the Comet Club. Comets are small, but they travel across the whole solar system."

Everyone agreed.

Week by week, the Comet Club learned together. Mr. Patel taught them how to find constellations, how to measure the brightness of stars, and how scientists discover new objects in space. Leo built a small model rocket that flew higher than the school roof. Daniel showed them how to use free software that let them see real images from professional telescopes around the world. Zoe made colorful charts of the moon's phases.

And Ava, slowly, began to talk.

She told them about her mother's telescope. She showed them the notebook where she had recorded the positions of stars for almost two years. Her friends were amazed.

"Ava, this is real data," Daniel said, flipping through the pages. "Scientists do exactly this."

For the first time in a long time, Ava felt proud.

In December, Mr. Patel came to the meeting looking excited. "I have a challenge for you," he said. "There's a national program where students help astronomers by looking at telescope images. You search for moving objects, like asteroids or comets, that the computers might have missed. Most people never find anything. But every image you check helps science."

The Comet Club signed up immediately.

Every Thursday, and many evenings at home, the four friends studied images on Daniel's laptop. The pictures showed thousands of tiny dots of light. Each set had several photos of the same patch of sky taken a few minutes apart. If a dot moved between the pictures while the stars stayed still, it might be something new.

It was slow, careful work. Most of the time, the moving dots turned out to be satellites, known asteroids, or just tiny errors in the camera. After a month, Leo started to lose interest.

"We're never going to find anything," he sighed. "Professional astronomers have giant telescopes. We're just kids with a laptop."

Ava thought about her notebook, and about all the nights she had spent on the cold garage roof, drawing tiny dots on paper.

"My mom used to say that science is mostly patience," she said quietly. "You keep looking, even when it's boring. Because the one time you stop looking might be the time something is there."

Leo looked at her for a moment. Then he pulled his chair back to the table. "Okay," he said. "Next image."

In February, on a snowy Thursday afternoon, it happened.

Ava was studying a set of four images from a telescope in Hawaii. At first, she saw nothing unusual. Then she noticed a tiny, faint smudge near the bottom corner. In the first picture, it was beside a bright star. In the second picture, it had moved slightly to the left. In the third and fourth, it moved again, in a perfect straight line.

Ava's heart began to pound. "Daniel," she whispered. "Can you check this one?"

Daniel zoomed in. Zoe leaned so close that her purple glasses nearly touched the screen. Leo stood up.

"It's moving," Daniel said slowly. "And it's not on the list of known objects."

Mr. Patel came over and studied the images for a long time. He checked the online databases twice. Then he looked up with a huge smile.

"I think," he said, "you may have found something."

They carefully filled out the report, recording the exact positions and times, just as Ava had done in her notebook for years. Mr. Patel submitted it to the program.

Then came the hardest part: waiting.

Weeks passed. The Comet Club continued their work, but every Thursday, the first question was always the same. "Any news?"

Finally, in early April, Mr. Patel walked into the meeting holding a printed email. His hands were shaking a little.

"Dear Comet Club of Riverside Middle School," he read aloud. "We are pleased to inform you that the object you reported has been confirmed by professional astronomers. It is a previously unknown asteroid in the main belt between Mars and Jupiter. Congratulations on your discovery."

For a second, the room was completely silent. Then Zoe screamed with joy, Leo jumped so high he almost hit the ceiling light, and Daniel spun his wheelchair in a happy circle. Ava just sat still, tears running down her face.

Mr. Patel continued reading. "As discoverers, you will have the opportunity to suggest a name for the asteroid, once its orbit is fully confirmed."

Everyone turned to Ava.

"You found it," Leo said. "You should choose."

Ava did not need to think for very long.

That night, she climbed onto the garage roof with her mother's telescope. The sky was clear and full of stars. Her father climbed up beside her, carrying two blankets and two mugs of hot chocolate, just as her mother used to.

"Have you decided on a name?" he asked gently.

Ava nodded. "I want to name it after Mom. Grace Brooks. That way, she'll always be up there, traveling around the sun."

Her father put his arm around her and was quiet for a long moment. "She would love that," he finally said, his voice soft.

Ava looked up at the sky, at the millions of stars shining in the darkness. Somewhere out there, too small to see, a tiny rock was traveling through space, carrying her mother's name.

"You were right, Mom," she whispered. "There was one waiting just for me."
