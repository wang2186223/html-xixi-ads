### chapter 1
Kites Over Maple Street

The new boy arrived on Maple Street on the first windy day of spring.

Lily Morgan saw the moving truck from her bedroom window. She watched as a family carried boxes into the empty blue house across the street. There was a mother, a father, a little girl about six years old, and a boy who looked about Lily's age, eleven or twelve. He was carrying something long and wrapped in brown paper, holding it very carefully, as if it were made of glass.

The next Monday, the boy appeared in Lily's classroom.

"Everyone, this is Kenji Tanaka," said Mr. Lewis, their teacher. "His family just moved here from Japan. Please make him feel welcome."

Kenji bowed slightly and said, "Hello. Nice to meet you." His English was careful and slow. Then he sat at the empty desk next to Lily and stared down at his hands.

At lunch, Kenji sat alone. Lily noticed that some kids looked at his lunch box, which was filled with rice, pickled vegetables, and a rolled omelet, and whispered to each other. Kenji closed the lid and did not eat.

Lily felt a little sick watching it. She remembered how it felt to be new. Two years ago, her family had moved from Texas, and for the first month, no one had talked to her at all. She picked up her tray and walked over.

"Can I sit here?" she asked.

Kenji looked up, surprised, and nodded.

"Your lunch looks really good," Lily said. "My mom only packs peanut butter sandwiches. Every single day."

Kenji smiled a little. "You want to try?" He opened his lunch box again and offered her a piece of the rolled omelet.

Lily tasted it. It was sweet and soft and delicious. "That's amazing," she said honestly. "What is it?"

"Tamagoyaki," Kenji said. "My grandmother's recipe."

That was the beginning of their friendship.

Over the next few weeks, Lily learned a lot about Kenji. He loved drawing, especially birds. He missed his grandmother, who still lived in a small town near the sea in Japan. And he was very worried about his English.

"When I speak, people wait too long," he told her one afternoon as they walked home. "I think of the words in Japanese first, then English. It is slow. Sometimes they stop listening."

"Your English is way better than my Japanese," Lily said. "I only know how to say tamagoyaki."

Kenji laughed. It was the first time Lily had heard him laugh, and it made her smile.

One Saturday, Lily knocked on the door of the blue house. Kenji's mother answered and invited her in. In the living room, on a large table, was the long object Lily had seen on moving day. Now it was unwrapped. It was a kite, but not like any kite Lily had ever seen. It was shaped like a giant bird with wide wings, painted in red, gold, and deep blue. Every feather was drawn by hand.

"Wow," Lily whispered.

"My grandmother made it," Kenji said proudly. "In her town, there is a kite festival every spring. Everyone makes kites and flies them together over the beach. She gave me this before we moved. She said, when I fly it, she will see it in her heart."

"Have you flown it here yet?"

Kenji shook his head. "I don't know where. And flying alone is not the same. At the festival, the whole sky is full of kites. It feels like the whole town is flying together."

Lily looked at the beautiful kite, then at her friend's sad face, and an idea began to grow in her mind.

"Kenji," she said slowly, "what if we had a kite festival here? On Maple Street?"

Kenji blinked. "Here?"

"Why not? There's the big park at the end of the street. It's always windy in April. We could invite everyone."

At first, the idea seemed impossible. But Lily was stubborn, and Kenji, once he started to believe, was full of plans. They talked to Mr. Lewis, who loved the idea and offered to turn it into a class art project. Every student would build their own kite. Kenji would teach them how.

The day Kenji stood in front of the class to explain kite making, his hands were shaking. Lily gave him a thumbs-up from her desk. Kenji took a deep breath and began.

He showed them how to make a frame from thin bamboo sticks, how to cut and glue the paper, and how to tie the string so the kite would balance in the wind. When he did not know an English word, he drew a picture on the board, and somehow everyone understood. By the end of the lesson, the whole class was asking him questions. Some of the same kids who had whispered about his lunch box were now begging him to help with their kites.

The next two weeks were full of glue, paper, paint, and laughter. Lily made a kite shaped like a yellow sun. Her friend Emma made a purple dragon. A boy named Jacob made a kite that looked like a slice of pizza, which made everyone laugh. Kenji's little sister, Hana, made a tiny pink butterfly kite with help from Lily.

Word spread. Parents heard about the project and wanted to join. The family who ran the corner bakery offered to make cookies shaped like kites. Kenji's mother and father cooked trays of rice balls and tamagoyaki to share. Even old Mr. Henderson, who usually yelled at kids for walking on his lawn, came out of his house carrying a kite he had made as a boy fifty years earlier.

On the last Saturday of April, the sky above Maple Park was bright blue, and the wind was perfect.

By ten o'clock, more than a hundred people had gathered on the grass. One by one, kites rose into the air: suns, dragons, fish, stars, butterflies, and one very silly slice of pizza. The sky filled with color.

Then Kenji stepped forward with his grandmother's kite.

Lily held the kite while Kenji held the string. He counted to three in Japanese, and Lily let go. The wind caught the great red-and-gold bird, and it climbed higher and higher, its painted wings spread wide, until it soared above every other kite in the sky.

Everyone stopped to watch. Someone began to clap, and soon the whole park was cheering.

Kenji's father filmed the moment on his phone and sent it to Japan. A few minutes later, his phone buzzed with a reply. Kenji read it, and his eyes filled with tears.

"What did she say?" Lily asked softly.

Kenji smiled, looking up at the bird flying high above Maple Street.

"She said she can see it," he said. "She said it looks like I am home."

Lily smiled too, because she knew that it was true.
