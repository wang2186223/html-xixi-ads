### chapter 1
The Robot Who Planted Sunflowers

Oliver Grant was the smartest kid in the seventh grade at Brookside Middle School, and he knew it. He could solve math problems faster than his teacher, write computer code in three languages, and build almost anything out of old electronics. What he could not do was work with other people.

"Group projects are inefficient," he told Ms. Rivera on the first day of the science fair assignment. "I can do it faster alone."

Ms. Rivera smiled patiently. "This year, every project must be done in teams of three. And every project must help our school community in some way." She looked at her list. "Oliver, you'll be working with Priya Sharma and Marcus Bell."

Oliver groaned quietly. Priya was the girl who spent every lunch break in the school garden, talking to tomato plants. Marcus was the class clown, famous for making the whole room laugh at exactly the wrong moments. Neither of them, Oliver thought, knew anything about science.

At their first meeting in the library, Oliver had already planned everything. "We'll build a robot," he said, opening his laptop to show a complicated design. "It will sort recycling automatically using a camera and machine learning. I'll do the coding and the building. You two can make the poster."

Priya frowned. "That sounds cool, but who would actually use it? Our school already sorts recycling pretty well."

"It's about the technology," Oliver said.

"Ms. Rivera said it should help the community," Marcus pointed out, spinning a pencil on his finger. "Not just look impressive."

Priya opened her notebook. "Actually, I have an idea. You know the school garden? It's dying. Mr. Thompson, the gardener, retired last spring, and nobody waters the plants properly anymore. The vegetables we grow are supposed to go to the food bank, but this year we barely grew anything."

Oliver raised an eyebrow. "So?"

"So what if we built a robot that takes care of the garden?" Priya said. "It could check if the soil is dry, water the plants, and tell us when something is wrong."

Oliver opened his mouth to argue, then closed it. The idea was actually interesting. Soil sensors, automatic watering, data tracking. It was a real problem with a real solution.

"Fine," he said. "But I'm still doing the coding."

Over the next six weeks, the three of them met every day after school. Oliver quickly discovered that he had been wrong about his teammates.

Priya knew an amazing amount about plants. She knew that tomatoes needed deep watering but hated wet leaves, that lettuce wilted in strong afternoon sun, and that sunflowers could grow taller than a basketball hoop if you gave them enough space. Without her knowledge, Oliver's robot would have watered everything the same way and probably drowned half the garden.

Marcus, it turned out, was brilliant with his hands. His uncle owned a bicycle repair shop, and Marcus had spent years fixing chains, wheels, and gears. When Oliver's robot kept getting stuck in the soft mud between garden rows, it was Marcus who solved it.

"You need wider wheels," he said, studying the problem. "Like a tractor. Narrow wheels sink." He came back the next day with two old bicycle tires and a box of tools. By the end of the afternoon, the robot rolled through the mud as smoothly as a car on a highway.

They named the robot Sunny, because Marcus insisted that every robot needs a friendly name. Sunny was about the size of a shopping cart, with a solar panel on its back, a water tank in its belly, and a long arm with a sensor that could test the moisture of the soil. Oliver wrote the code that let Sunny move along the rows, check each plant, and decide how much water it needed.

Of course, not everything went smoothly.

The first time they tested Sunny, a bug in Oliver's code made the robot spin in circles and spray water everywhere, soaking all three of them. Oliver was furious with himself, but Marcus laughed so hard that he fell into a pile of compost. Then Priya started laughing too. After a moment, even Oliver smiled.

"Okay," he admitted, wiping water off his glasses. "Maybe I need someone to test my code."

The second problem was bigger. Two weeks before the science fair, someone left the garden gate open, and a family of rabbits ate half of the young lettuce plants. Priya was heartbroken.

"All that work," she said quietly, kneeling beside the chewed stems.

Oliver thought for a long time that night. The next morning, he arrived with a new idea. "What if Sunny could detect animals? I can add a motion sensor. When it sees something moving at night, it plays a sound and flashes a light. Not to hurt them, just to scare them away gently."

Marcus grinned. "Can the sound be a dog barking?"

"It can be anything you want," Oliver said.

They tested it that weekend. When a curious rabbit hopped toward the garden, Sunny's lights blinked and a recording of Marcus doing his best dog impression echoed across the yard. The rabbit ran straight back into the bushes. The three friends cheered so loudly that a neighbor looked over the fence.

As the weeks passed, the garden came back to life. The tomatoes turned red and heavy. The beans climbed their poles. And along the fence, a row of sunflowers that Priya had planted grew taller and taller, their golden faces following the sun across the sky.

Oliver noticed something else changing too. He started looking forward to the afternoons in the garden. He liked hearing Priya explain why bees loved purple flowers. He liked Marcus's terrible jokes. For the first time, he realized that the best ideas did not always come from one brilliant person working alone. Sometimes they came from three very different people sharing what they knew.

On the day of the science fair, the gym was filled with impressive projects: volcanoes, solar cars, and a model of the solar system that actually rotated. But when the judges reached the table with Sunny, they stopped for a long time.

Priya showed them the charts of how much water the garden had saved. Marcus demonstrated how the wide wheels handled rough ground. Oliver explained the code and the animal sensor. Then they showed the judges a photo of three large boxes of fresh vegetables, all donated to the local food bank.

Brookside Middle School's garden team won first place.

But what Oliver remembered most was not the trophy. It was what happened afterward, when the three of them walked out to the garden at sunset. Sunny was rolling slowly down the rows, its solar panel catching the last light. The sunflowers stood tall above the fence, glowing gold.

"Same team next year?" Marcus asked.

Oliver looked at his two friends and smiled.

"I wouldn't want any other team," he said.
