Chapter 1

The Lighthouse on Gull Island

Maya Chen had never wanted to spend her summer on an island with no internet, no shopping mall, and exactly one grocery store that closed at six o'clock. But her parents had both taken jobs overseas for three months, and Grandpa Leo was the only family member who could take her in. So on a gray morning in June, fourteen-year-old Maya stepped off the ferry onto Gull Island with a backpack, a suitcase, and a very bad attitude.

Grandpa Leo was waiting at the dock. He was tall and thin, with a white beard and a yellow raincoat that looked older than Maya herself. He smiled so warmly that she almost forgot to be grumpy.

"Welcome to the edge of the world," he said, taking her suitcase. "I hope you like seagulls. They have opinions about everything."

Grandpa Leo lived in a small white cottage next to the old lighthouse at the north end of the island. The lighthouse had not been used for nearly twenty years. Modern ships used satellite navigation now, and the town council had switched off the great lamp long ago. Its tower was faded, its windows were cracked, and its iron stairs groaned whenever the wind blew.

"Why do you still live out here?" Maya asked on her first night, as they ate fish soup at the small kitchen table.

Grandpa Leo looked out the window at the dark tower. "I was the keeper for thirty-one years," he said. "Every night, I climbed those stairs and made sure the light was shining. A lighthouse is a promise, Maya. It tells every sailor out there that someone is watching, and that they are not alone."

Maya did not answer, but she thought about those words as she fell asleep.

The first week was boring. Maya walked along the rocky beach, read the three books she had brought, and complained about the slow phone signal. But on the eighth day, she found Grandpa Leo on a ladder inside the lighthouse, scraping old paint off the walls.

"What are you doing?" she asked.

"The town is celebrating the island's two-hundredth birthday in August," he said. "I asked the council if I could fix up the lighthouse for the festival. They said yes, as long as I don't fall off a ladder." He winked. "Want to help?"

Maya almost said no. Then she noticed how slowly he climbed down, and how his hands shook a little when he held the scraper. She picked up a brush.

"Fine," she said. "But only because you might fall off the ladder."

Over the next few weeks, the lighthouse became Maya's project too. She scraped, sanded, and painted until her arms ached. She learned how to replace a broken window pane, how to oil the rusty hinges of the heavy door, and how to clean the giant glass lens at the top of the tower. The lens was made of hundreds of curved glass pieces arranged like a beehive. When the afternoon sun hit it, rainbows danced across the walls.

"This lens can throw light more than twenty miles across the water," Grandpa Leo told her proudly. "It was made in France in 1890. They don't build them like this anymore."

Maya also started to learn about the island. She met Mrs. Patel, who ran the grocery store and gave her free peaches. She met Ben, a boy her age who helped his mother on their fishing boat, the Silver Fin. Ben showed her where the seals slept on the rocks and how to tell the weather by looking at the clouds.

"Mackerel sky, not twenty-four hours dry," he said one afternoon, pointing at a pattern of small, scaly clouds. "That means rain is coming."

By August, the lighthouse looked brand new. Its walls shone white, its red door gleamed, and the lens sparkled like a diamond. Grandpa Leo had even repaired the old electric motor that turned the lamp. The only thing left was the festival, when the mayor would switch the light on for one special night.

But two days before the festival, the weather changed.

It started with a strange stillness in the afternoon. The seagulls stopped crying. The sea turned the color of slate. Then the wind arrived, howling across the island and bending the trees sideways. Rain hammered on the cottage roof. The radio crackled with a storm warning.

At eight o'clock, the power went out across the whole island.

Maya and Grandpa Leo lit candles in the kitchen. Then Grandpa Leo's old marine radio, which ran on batteries, buzzed to life. A voice came through the static.

"This is the Silver Fin. Our navigation system is down. We can't see the harbor. Does anyone copy?"

Maya's heart jumped. "That's Ben's boat!"

Grandpa Leo grabbed the radio. "Silver Fin, this is Leo at the north lighthouse. Hold your position. We will give you a light."

"But the power is out," Maya said. "The motor won't work."

Grandpa Leo was already pulling on his yellow raincoat. "The lamp has a backup battery for emergencies. We tested it last week, remember? And if the motor won't turn the lens, we'll turn it by hand."

They ran through the storm to the lighthouse. The wind nearly knocked Maya off her feet, but Grandpa Leo held her arm tightly. Inside, they climbed the spiral stairs, one hundred and twelve steps, all the way to the top.

Grandpa Leo connected the backup battery, and the lamp flickered on. Its bright beam shot through the lens and out into the darkness. But without the motor, the light stayed still, pointing only in one direction.

"The ships need to see it flash," Grandpa Leo said, breathing hard. "That's how they know which lighthouse it is."

Maya found the old hand crank on the side of the lens. It was stiff, but she had oiled it herself only a week before. She pushed with all her strength, and slowly, the great lens began to turn. Around and around the light swept, cutting through the rain like a golden sword.

For forty minutes, Maya turned the crank. Her arms burned and her shoulders ached, but she did not stop. Grandpa Leo kept the radio close, guiding the boat with calm instructions.

Finally, the radio crackled again. "Silver Fin here. We see the harbor. We're safe. Thank you, lighthouse."

Maya let go of the crank and sat down on the cold floor, laughing and crying at the same time. Grandpa Leo knelt beside her and hugged her.

"You kept the promise," he said quietly.

Two nights later, the festival went ahead under a clear sky full of stars. The whole island gathered on the cliff. But when the mayor stepped forward to switch on the light, she turned to Maya instead.

"I think this honor belongs to someone else," she said.

Maya pressed the switch. The great lamp blazed to life, sweeping across the sea, and everyone cheered. Ben and his mother stood at the front, clapping the loudest.

At the end of the summer, Maya stood at the ferry dock once more. This time, she did not want to leave.

"Will you come back next year?" Grandpa Leo asked.

Maya looked up at the lighthouse, shining white against the blue sky.

"Every summer," she said. "Someone has to keep the light on."
