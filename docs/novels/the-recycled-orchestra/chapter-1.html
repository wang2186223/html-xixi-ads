Chapter 1

The Recycled Orchestra

The music room at Lincoln Middle School had been closed for two years. There was no money for new instruments, and the old ones were broken. The violins had snapped strings, the drums had holes, and the piano was so out of tune that it sounded like a cat walking across the keys. When the school's last music teacher retired, nobody replaced her.

For twelve-year-old Rosa Delgado, this was the saddest thing in the world.

Rosa loved music more than anything. She hummed while she did her homework, tapped rhythms on her desk during math class, and fell asleep every night listening to old recordings of orchestras. Her grandfather had played the guitar in a band when he was young, and he had taught her the names of every note. But her family could not afford music lessons or a real instrument.

One afternoon in September, Rosa was taking out the trash at home when she noticed something. When she tapped an empty plastic bucket with a stick, it made a deep, round boom. When she hit a metal pot lid, it rang like a bell. And when she blew across the top of an empty glass bottle, it made a soft, low whistle.

Rosa stood in the backyard for a long time, tapping and blowing and listening. An idea began to grow in her mind.

The next day, she showed her best friend, Marcus, a video she had found online. It was about a town in South America where children built instruments out of garbage from a giant landfill. They made violins from old cans, cellos from oil barrels, and flutes from water pipes. Then they formed an orchestra and played beautiful music around the world.

"We could do that," Rosa said, her eyes shining. "We could build our own orchestra."

Marcus looked doubtful. "Out of trash?"

"Out of recycled things," Rosa corrected. "Things people throw away."

Together, they went to see the principal, Mrs. Okafor. She listened carefully to their idea. Then she smiled.

"You can use the old music room after school," she said. "And I know someone who might help."

That someone turned out to be Mr. Diaz, the school janitor. He was a quiet man with gray hair and strong hands, and he had been fixing things at Lincoln Middle School for twenty years. When Rosa explained the plan, his eyes lit up.

"When I was a boy in Mexico," he said, "we didn't have money for toys. I built everything myself. Let me see what I can do."

Rosa and Marcus put up posters around the school. "Join the Recycled Orchestra! No experience needed. Bring your old junk and your imagination!"

On the first day, seven students came. By the second week, there were fifteen. Each of them brought something: cardboard tubes, old cans, rubber bands, broken umbrellas, plastic bottles, bicycle parts, and wooden boxes.

The music room turned into a workshop. Mr. Diaz brought his toolbox and showed them how to cut, sand, and glue safely. A girl named Lily, who loved science, explained how sound worked.

"Sound is just vibration," she said. "A long string makes a low sound. A short string makes a high sound. A big drum makes a deep sound. A small drum makes a higher one."

Slowly, the instruments came to life.

Marcus built a drum set from plastic buckets, paint cans, and an old cookie tin. Lily made a xylophone from different lengths of copper pipe, each one tuned by cutting it a little shorter. Two brothers, Sam and David, built shakers by filling bottles with rice, beans, and tiny pebbles. A shy boy named Kevin, who almost never spoke in class, turned an old cigar box, a broom handle, and four guitar strings into a small guitar that actually played real notes.

Rosa's instrument was the hardest. She wanted to make a violin. With Mr. Diaz's help, she used a large, flat cookie tin for the body, a piece of wood from an old chair for the neck, and real violin strings that her grandfather bought for her as a gift. It took her five weeks. The first time she drew the bow across the strings, it made a terrible screeching sound, and everyone covered their ears. But Rosa kept practicing. Every afternoon, she practiced scales until her fingers hurt. And one day, the screech became a song.

The orchestra still had one problem. Nobody knew how to lead it. They could each make sounds, but together they sounded like a traffic jam.

Then Rosa's grandfather came to visit.

He stood at the door of the music room, listening to the noisy practice with a big smile. Then he walked to the front of the room, picked up a pencil, and tapped it on a music stand.

"Musicians," he said in a warm voice, "an orchestra is like a family. Everyone must listen to each other. The drums keep the heartbeat. The shakers are the breath. The strings sing the story. Now, let us try again. Slowly."

Grandpa Delgado came every Tuesday and Thursday after that. He taught them to count together, to start and stop at the same time, and to play softly when someone else had the melody. He arranged simple songs they all knew: folk songs, holiday songs, and even a slow version of a famous classical piece.

By December, the Recycled Orchestra sounded like real music.

Mrs. Okafor asked them to perform at the winter concert. Rosa was so nervous that she could barely eat that day. When the curtain opened, the school gym was packed with parents, teachers, and students. Some people laughed quietly when they saw the instruments made of buckets and cans. Rosa's hands trembled on her cookie tin violin.

Then Grandpa Delgado raised his hands, and the orchestra began to play.

Marcus's bucket drums started a steady beat. The shakers joined in, soft as rain. Lily's copper pipes rang out like bells. Kevin's little guitar strummed gentle chords. And then Rosa lifted her bow and played the melody, high and clear and sweet, floating above everything else.

The laughter stopped. The gym became completely silent, except for the music.

When the last note faded, there was a moment of stillness. Then the whole audience stood up and cheered. Some parents were wiping tears from their eyes. Mr. Diaz clapped so hard that his hands turned red.

A week later, a local newspaper wrote a story about the Recycled Orchestra. Soon, a music store in the city offered to donate ten real instruments to the school. A retired music teacher volunteered to give free lessons. And in January, the old music room officially reopened, with a new sign on the door that Kevin had painted himself: "Lincoln Music Room. Everyone Welcome."

But Rosa never stopped playing her cookie tin violin.

"Why don't you use one of the new violins?" Marcus asked her one day.

Rosa looked at her strange little instrument, with its bent tin body and its old chair-wood neck.

"Because this one taught me something," she said. "Music isn't about having the perfect instrument. It's about having something to say, and people to say it with."
